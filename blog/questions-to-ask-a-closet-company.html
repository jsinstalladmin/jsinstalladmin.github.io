12 Questions to Ask Before You Sign With a Closet Company

Most of these take ten seconds to ask and will tell you more than any showroom.

4 min readUpdated 2026-11-06

On who actually does the work

  1. Does the person who measures my room also draw it? If the answer is no, something gets lost between the two.
  2. Do your installers work for you, or are they subcontracted? Subcontracted crews are paid by the job, which rewards speed over care.
  3. Will the same crew that installs it come back if something is wrong?

On the quote

  1. Is this price installed, or is installation separate?
  2. Does it include removing the existing shelving?
  3. Does it include patching the holes you drill?
  4. What happens to the price if my walls are out of square? Every house is. The answer should be nothing.
  5. Is there a deposit, and when is it due? Ours is after you have seen a drawing and a fixed price, not before.

On the materials

  1. What is the core material and how thick is it? Three-quarter-inch is the answer you want. Half-inch sags.
  2. Is every exposed edge banded? Unbanded edges swell the first time they get wet.
  3. What is the lead time, and does it change by finish? Gloss finishes genuinely take longer. A company that quotes the same lead time for everything has not checked.

The one that matters most

Ask them what they would not do in your closet. A designer who only agrees with you is selling. A designer who tells you a wired LED strip cannot go on an adjustable shelf, or that a shelf past 36 inches will bow, is building.

We will tell you that before you pay for it, which occasionally costs us a sale and has never once cost us a callback.

What a good answer sounds like

The questions are easy. Knowing whether you have been answered is the harder part, so here is what we would listen for on four of them.

"Do your installers work for you?" A good answer is a plain yes or a plain no. A bad answer is "they are our partners" or "we have worked with them for years", which is a subcontractor with better wording. Subcontracted crews are paid per job, and a crew paid per job is paid to leave.

"What happens if my walls are out of square?" The answer should be: nothing, we scribe to them. If the answer involves a possible extra charge on the day, the quote you are holding is not a fixed price, because every house is out of square somewhere.

"How thick is the board?" You want three-quarters of an inch. If you hear five eighths or half, that is a different product and it belongs at a different price. If you hear a brand name instead of a number, ask again.

"What is the lead time?" A real answer varies by finish, because stock white and a high-gloss import do not arrive on the same day. One lead time quoted for every finish means nobody has checked.

Four things that should make you slow down

  1. A discount that expires today. Closets are not priced by the hour and nothing about the cost changes overnight. A price that only exists while the salesperson is in your hallway is a pressure tactic, not a price.
  2. A quote with no drawing. A number on its own is not a quote, it is an estimate with confidence. You should be able to see an elevation of every wall with dimensions on it before you pay anything.
  3. A large deposit before the design is settled. Ours is due after you have seen a drawing and a fixed price. Before that, there is nothing to deposit against.
  4. Agreement with everything you suggest. If you propose a 48-inch shelf span and nobody mentions that it will bow, you have found a company that sells closets rather than one that builds them.

And one question to ask yourself

What are you actually storing? Most closets are designed around how many clothes somebody owns and not around what kind. Forty pairs of shoes and forty shirts need almost nothing in common from the cabinetry.

Spend ten minutes counting before anybody visits: how many long hanging items, how many short, roughly how many folded stacks, how many pairs of shoes, and whether anything bulky has to live in there. That list is worth more at the measure than any number of reference photos, and it is the thing we will ask you for first.

Want to answer them for yourself? Build your own room in the 3D designer. It will not let you draw a shelf that sags, it tells you which sections your door type will block, and the price comes off the same parts list we order from. Then ask us the twelve questions anyway.
